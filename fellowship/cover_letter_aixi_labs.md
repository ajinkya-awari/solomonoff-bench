Dear Marcus,

AIXI requires Solomonoff induction as its context model. Solomonoff induction is incomputable. The open engineering question is whether a modern LLM can fill that role in practice — and if not, how large the gap is and whether it grows with the algorithmic complexity of the input.

I spent the last week building a reproducible pilot benchmark to begin answering this. Here is what I found.

---

## What I built

**SolomonoffBench** — 300 binary sequences generated by Turing machines with formally computed program lengths (24–60 bits) across four complexity levels. Two free Qwen2.5 base models scored on Kaggle T4 GPUs at zero cost using direct forward-pass logits. The metric is gzip-normalized excess code length (EL_gzip) — a pilot proxy for the Solomonoff Gap while CTW is not yet implemented.

Code and paper source: https://github.com/ajinkya-awari/solomonoff-bench  
Full 4-page preprint: https://github.com/ajinkya-awari/solomonoff-bench/blob/master/paper/main.tex  
*(arXiv submission in progress)*

---

## The result you actually want to know

Neither tested model comes close to gzip compression performance on these sequences.

| Model | EL_gzip range (bits/sym above gzip) | vs. random ceiling |
|-------|--------------------------------------|---------------------|
| Qwen2.5-3B (3.1B params) | 0.917 – 0.972 | Below (beats random) |
| Qwen2.5-1.5B (1.5B params) | 1.028 – 1.096 | Above (worse than random) |
| gzip baseline | 0.000 | — |
| random predictor | 1.000 | — |

The larger model at least beats random prediction. Neither model approaches gzip. More importantly, both show near-flat profiles across complexity levels 24–60 bits (spread ≤ 0.068 bits/sym), meaning the models are insensitive to the algorithmic complexity gradient entirely.

This is the Week 1 answer: **no, current free LLMs cannot substitute for Solomonoff induction, not even against a trivial compression baseline.**

The honest caveat: incremental gzip at 100-symbol windows is too coarse to be a theoretically meaningful baseline. CTW is the correct comparison, and that is exactly what Week 2 will deliver.

## Why I am applying

The Week 2 upgrade is the real research contribution:

1. Implement CTW (Willems, Shtarkov & Tjalkens 1995) in Python — validated against known sequences (uniform → 1.0 bit/sym, all-zeros → 0, alternating → 0)
2. Run at depths D=4, 8, 12 and take the minimum as the baseline (gap against the best CTW, not a fixed-depth one)
3. Compute the CTW-normalized Solomonoff Gap: SG(M, x) = H(M, x) − H_CTW(x)
4. Extend to the full 2,400-sequence dataset (adding CA sequences, LFSR sequences, canary contamination controls)
5. Produce the headline number you actually want: at what program-bit threshold does SG exceed 0.5 bits/sym?

The fellowship would fund this work over the next 4–8 weeks while I prepare for my MSc CS at Edinburgh (September 2026 intake, GREAT Scholarship secured).

---

## Why this matters for AIXI research

If SG grows monotonically with program bits, it gives a formal, empirically grounded answer to the AIXI-LLM substitution question: the threshold K* at which practical LLMs become useless as Solomonoff approximators. If SG is near zero at low complexity levels, it suggests hybrid AIXI-LLM architectures are viable for the simpler end of the algorithmic complexity spectrum.

Either answer is useful. The benchmark is designed to produce a concrete number rather than a qualitative claim.

---

## What I am not claiming

- This is not a paper about scaling laws (two data points is not a law)
- EL_gzip and SG are not the same metric — the paper never conflates them
- The K values are machine-specific (K_M), not universal Kolmogorov complexity
- CTW is not the true Solomonoff bound, but its best known practical approximation for binary sequences
